The shared garden water channel

Nima leads Rook along four garden paths with narrow water channels; one flower bed is dry while trees, a pool, and vegetables share the water.

Four paths divided Nima’s garden, with water glimmering beside them. One bed of flowers looked thirsty. Rook pointed to the nearest channel. “Could we make this one larger?” Nima asked him to walk the whole garden first. They visited trees, a kitchen plot, a shaded pool, and the thirsty bed. The water served many places before it reached the flowers Rook had noticed.

Nima checking the safe maintenance point

Rook holds a leaf basket as Nima works at a safe maintenance point, with downstream garden beds visible along the channel.

At the far corner, leaves had collected where the keeper could safely clear them. Nima arranged the maintenance and explained who depended on the flow downstream. Rook held a basket for the leaves. “I was ready to change the part I could see,” he admitted. “Seeing a little is useful,” Nima replied, “when it makes us ask about the rest.”

Rook's lantern beside the returning water

Water moves back into the dry flower bed while a child counts ripples and neighboring gardeners inspect their sections with Nima.

Water returned to the flowers gradually. Rook watched impatiently, then noticed that the pool was settling and a child was counting new ripples. Nothing was broken because it took time. Nima asked each gardener to check their own bed before calling the job finished. A kitchen grower found another small obstruction, and the keepers added it to their maintenance notes.

The Garden of Many Doors / CHAPTER 02PAGE 4 / 4
The sketch of four garden paths

Rook sketches the interconnected garden under a sunset lamp, with Nima’s chosen credit beside the paths and a sealed note addressed to Moss.

At sunset the paths looked unchanged, but Rook understood them differently. With Nima’s approval, he sketched their shared flow and credited the garden keepers. The drawing was a story about cooperation, not instructions for building a water system. Beneath it he wrote: Walk farther before moving the nearest stone. He sent Moss the page, certain that a bridge keeper would appreciate a garden keeper’s question.
