# Rook's field journal

Rook lives in **Lantern Harbor**, a fictional city of bridges, roof gardens and
stone gateways. Nobody there agrees on the best route through town. Rook's gift
is listening long enough to help someone find their own.

He is a small red bird with two swept crest feathers, one white eye patch, a dark
scarf, a cream satchel and a brass lantern. He has wings and small bird feet.
His head preserves the silhouette of the project's existing mark. The scarf,
satchel and lamp belong to his story; the mark remains simple.

## His rule

**A good route leaves the next traveler a choice.**

Rook helps rather than commands. He lends useful tools, explains his map and
admits when he does not know the way. He is brave enough to begin again and kind
enough to bring someone along. He is neither a magic privacy shield nor a
technical promise. The stories are fiction, separate from product documentation.

## Volume one: Little ways forward

1. **The borrowed light.** A rabbit courier cannot deliver the last letters after
   the harbor lamp goes dark. Rook shares his lantern, then leaves it with the
   courier. Theme: access is something you can share.
2. **The noisy market.** A hedgehog mapmaker cannot hear a customer through a
   forest of fluttering signs. Rook helps untangle the stall and discovers a sign
   the mapmaker still needs. Theme: a quieter path needs thoughtful choices.
3. **The bridge that waited.** Tess, an otter traveler with a green backpack, reaches a broken footbridge on her way to a train. Rook
   checks a plank, decides to turn back, and helps build a marked detour with the
   neighbors. Theme: restarting is part of moving forward.

Every chapter has six illustrated panels and a completed story. Read left page
top to bottom, then right page top to bottom. Narration and dialogue are real
HTML beneath the art, not small baked-in image text. The text edition describes
the action and gives all dialogue in order.

## Chapters 4 and 5: bridges and choice

**The two bridges:** Pip needs to cross a wooden bridge and then a stone bridge. Rook keeps the first gate closed until he and Moss finish and check the far bridge. His lantern gives the signal. Theme: check the far bridge before opening the near gate.

**The right material:** A garden crossing, a heavy mail cart and a windy gorge call for different bridges. Wood, stone and iron each find a useful place. Theme: choose the bridge that fits the journey. The materials are a metaphor for choices, not a ranking of VPN security or a protocol-to-material equivalence.

## The atlas opens: five connected worlds

The expanded collection is **five worlds, ten completed chapters in each**.
Every chapter has four individually narrated pages, a complete central arc,
a summary, a theme, and scene descriptions. The full original text is in
`content/worlds.json`; `WORLDS.md` contains the chapter index and recurring cast.
The five existing comics remain the opening five Lantern Harbor adventures,
linked by `legacyChapter` rather than replaced by the new retellings.

### Where Rook came from

Lumen found Rook drawn beside an unfinished route in an old atlas. During a storm,
a little lantern oil touched the margin. The bird blinked and stepped onto the
desk when a stranded courier needed help. He remembers the library and his rule,
but not an author or a predetermined destination. That uncertainty remains
honest throughout the stories.

Lumen offered him a name, a scarf, and a satchel. Rook chose how to use them.
His lantern is ordinary brass; it needs oil and care and illuminates a limited
area. The book does not magically answer questions. Its loose blank leaf holds
invitations, credited accounts, and questions the traveler has actually asked.

Rook is a gentleman through conduct: he asks before entering, respects a private
letter, shares credit, gives help without a debt, and corrects a mistake. He can
feel disappointed or impatient. His growth comes from learning alongside people
with knowledge and agency of their own.

### The journey

1. **Lantern Harbor** establishes Pip, Moss, Lumen, and the travelers' shelf. A
   borrowed light, checked crossings, privacy, an inventor's rescue, and an
   unfinished atlas teach Rook how to begin. Aster the camel joins the departure.
2. **The Garden of Many Doors** draws narrow cultural inspiration from Iranian
   and Persian garden, book, craft, and exchange motifs. Sima, Nima, Darya,
   Leila, and Soraya lead the local adventures. Soraya is a princess astronomer
   whose rescue plan involves a caretaker and the frightened dragon Zephyr.
3. **The River of Returning Lanterns** brings Rook to Mei's printing workshop,
   Lin's bridge conversations, Shen's ferries, Jun's kite, and a contemporary
   roof garden. Craft histories and present lives share the horizon. A train
   carries the next introduction north.
4. **The Snowbound Library** visits one fictional northern community during
   winter. Dasha, Anya, Misha, Lev, Vera, and an original talking loaf named Bun
   welcome him. Borrowed felt boots and an earflap hat serve the weather while
   keeping Rook's red face, beak, and white eye patch recognizable. A coastal ship carries him onward.
5. **The Wide-Sky Commons** visits Jo's archive, a public meeting, an accessible
   crossing, a community broadcast, and Rosa's airfield. A checked U.S. Bill of
   Rights display includes the Second Amendment alongside the other amendments.
   The final atlas connects invitations with consent, and a flight and local
   ferry return Rook to a home that has kept living in his absence.

Every place is invented. Cultural notes cite a small set of checked primary
sources in `content/cultural-sources.json`. The fictional geography, mixed travel
technologies, names, dialogue, and conflicts are original. No country, people,
faith, or ordinary resident is cast as a collective villain. Rook never arrives
to complete a culture or claim it as his discovery.

### Dragons, towers, and courage

Mira's watchtower adventure is the inventor's plan, the trained crew's work,
and Rook's useful errands. Soraya's observatory adventure gives the princess
her questions, her notes, her telescope, and her role in resolving the problem.
Willow seeks a quieter place to rest; Flint must stop demanding travelers'
private histories and learn the limits of his gatekeeping role. These conflicts
are particular fictional circumstances. They do not map a nation's population
onto a monster.

Courage includes saying that a route is unknown, waiting when a keeper closes a
crossing, asking who can actually use a design, and making a public correction.
The stories contain no weapon how-to, hazardous repair directions, or promises
that a tool removes every barrier. The brand's bridges remain open metaphors,
with accurate product architecture described separately.

### Reader and narrator

Each narrated chapter has four unique page texts, **57–71 words per page**.
The 200-page collection contains **12,879 narration words**. Dialogue is already
inside the page text and should not be invented during playback. Its scene
description supports alt text; its `focus` names an element suitable for a
gentle visual emphasis while that page is read.

Play begins with an explicit reader action. Page progression may continue
within the selected chapter. At its ending, narration stops and the reader
chooses what follows. A chapter is complete even if the reader never opens the
next one. Text, scene descriptions, and ordinary navigation remain available
without sound or animation. Respect reduced motion and give pause, stop, and
previous/next controls clear names.

The guide can accompany a task on the website or in the mobile app without
obscuring the actual task's progress. Rook's helper role uses clear, brief copy
and a few deliberate poses. Story playback is a choice, and his expressions
never turn an uncertain technical outcome into a success claim.
